## Description

_Statistics_ is the **science of interpreting data to answer real-world questions**: from tomorrow's weather to why a supermarket is organized in a certain way.

### Types

_Descriptive statistics_ collects, organizes, and presents data using tables and graphs to visually identify the most important aspects of a dataset. This is the type of statistics you’ll use most often in everyday analysis.

_Inferential statistics_ uses a representative sample to draw conclusions about an entire population. If you want to learn about a characteristic of an entire country, you don’t survey every resident, you take a sample and use it to make inferences or **predictions**.

## Details

### Variables

_Descriptive statistics_ begins with two simple questions: **Who or what are you studying, and what do you want to measure about that person, object, or phenomenon?**

This distinction between an _individual_ and a _variable_ is the foundation for organizing data, choosing graphs, and drawing useful conclusions—and it applies whether you’re analyzing friends or suitcase sales.

#### What is an individual, and what is a variable in statistics?

**When you collect data, you first identify who you’re observing. That subject of study is the** _individual_, and it isn’t necessarily a person.

**What is an** _individual_ **in statistics? It is the unit you are studying**. It can be a person, a suitcase, an ice cream flavor, a soccer team, or a medication. It is defined by the context of the analysis, not by its nature.

The _variables_ are the characteristics of those _individuals_, such as color, height, weight, age, size, etc.

![[variables_vs_individuals.png]]

#### Types of variables

_Variables_ **are divided into two main categories based on what they describe about an** _individual_. This classification determines what calculations you can perform later.

##### Quantitative Variables: Discrete and Continuous

_Quantitative or numerical variables_ are expressed as **numbers and allow for calculations** such as the mean, maximum, or minimum. Within this category, there are two subtypes:

- _Discrete_: These are counted in whole numbers. You don’t sell half a suitcase; you sell 3 or 4.
- _Continuous_: These allow for decimals. A person’s height can be 1.68, 1.72, or 1.9999 meters.

##### Qualitative Variables: Nominal and Ordinal

_Qualitative or categorical variables_ **describe an individual** without an underlying numerical calculation. They also come in two forms:

- _Nominal_: They have no order. Hair color, whether a suitcase has wheels or not .
- _Ordinal_: They follow a hierarchy. Cold, warm, or hot. Low, medium, or high.

#### Correlation vs Causation

_Statistics_ is a powerful tool that allows us to analyze the relationship between two _variables_, but it is essential to distinguish between _correlation_ and _causation_. 

##### Correlation

_Correlation_ **indicates that two variables may exhibit similar or related behavior**. However, this does not imply that a change in one _variable_ is the direct cause of a change in the other.

###### How can you detect a correlation?

To examine _correlations_, we often use graphs that illustrate the relationship between two data distributions. These graphs make it easier to identify patterns and similar behaviors among the _variables_. But it’s crucial to remember that **these similarities may be coincidences or the result of other factors not considered in the analysis**.

###### Can a correlation imply causation?

A classic example of a _correlation_ that also involves _causation_ is that between hours of sleep and fatigue. If you sleep fewer hours, you are likely to feel more fatigued. Here, we not only observe a _correlational_ relationship but can also confidently state that one _variable_ (fewer hours of sleep) has a direct effect on another (greater fatigue).


##### Why is this distinction important?

When analyzing data, it’s vital to distinguish between _correlation_ and _causation_ to avoid drawing erroneous conclusions. Understanding this difference allows us to be more critical of the data and make meaningful and accurate inferences. So the next time you come across a graph showing a similar trend between two _variables_, remember to **ask yourself whether there’s truly a causal effect or just an apparent** _correlation_.


### Data Tables

#### One-dimensional

This is a table where **a single variable is enough to find the data**. If your query has only one condition (for example, sales by month), you are dealing with a one-dimensional table.

![[one-dimensional_table.png]]


#### Two-dimensional
When **your question involves two variables at the same time**, a flat table falls short.

If you want to know which November had the highest sales, you’re already cross-referencing month and year, and **you need a structure with rows and columns that combine both dimensions**.

By expanding the original table to include each of the four years, you can see that November 2021 was the month with the highest sales. And when you visualize the data, additional patterns emerge: November consistently shows the highest sales each year, while May and June were low between 2018 and 2020 before rebounding in 2021.

![[two-dimensional_table.png]]

![[two-dimensional_table_graph.png]]

#### How to tell what kind of table we need?

**The rule of thumb is to count the questions. If you need just one question to get the data, the table is** _one-dimensional_**. If you need two, it's** _two-dimensional_.

For example, “What were the total sales for January?” can be answered with a single variable. On the other hand, “What were the sales for November?” requires a follow-up question: “In total, or for which year?” That second question reveals that the data is organized in a two-dimensional structure.
